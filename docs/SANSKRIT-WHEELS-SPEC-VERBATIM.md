Yes. I think we've been making a mistake: we've been organising wheels around grammar topics instead of the things you actually want to say.

You don't need a Case Wheel, Pronoun Wheel, Agreement Wheel and Verb Wheel separately. All four can be learned by changing the rings of a single sentence.

I'd condense our 15 families into just 4 master wheels, each with progressive levels.

## The four master wheels

## 1. Conversation

Speaking

Ask → Answer → Follow up

Five rings: Person · Question · Topic · Answer · Follow-up

Bhavān kutra gacchati?\
Where are you going?

Ahaṃ vārāṇasīṃ gacchāmi.\
I'm going to Varanasi.

Absorbs: questions, ka–ta–ya families, pronouns, numbers, relative words and everyday vocabulary.

## 2. Expression

I think / like / want → X → because → Y

Five rings: Speaker · Attitude · Subject · Connector · Reason

Saṃskṛtaṃ sundaram iti manye, yataḥ tat madhuram asti.

I think Sanskrit is beautiful because it is sweet-sounding.

Absorbs: opinions, preferences, desires, reasons, comparisons, conditions, philosophical logic and particles.

## 3. Action

Who → Does what → To whom/what → Where → When

Five rings: Actor · Action · Object · Location · Time

Guruḥ śiṣyāya pustakaṃ dadāti.

The teacher gives the student a book.

Absorbs: cases, conjugations, singular/dual/plural, commands, tense, causatives, prefixes and action sequences.

## 4. Description

This → Thing → Is/has → Quality → Relationship

Five rings: Demonstrative · Noun · Property · Degree · Relationship

Idaṃ pustakaṃ sundaram asti.

This book is beautiful.

Absorbs: gender, noun endings, adjective agreement, possessives, comparisons, numbers and eventually compounds.

That's enough. Four meaningful ways to use language.

## How the levels should progress

Instead of introducing lots of new wheels, we progressively make the same four wheels more expressive.

| Level | What changes                               |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------ |
| 1     | Simple present-tense sentences             |
| 2     | Questions and negatives                    |
| 3     | Different people and numbers               |
| 4     | Locations, objects and relationships       |
| 5     | Past, future and commands                  |
| 6     | Reasons, preferences and comparisons       |
| 7     | Conditions, sequences and relative clauses |
| 8     | Complex Sanskrit and Trika expressions     |

Importantly, these aren't mandatory identical levels across every family. Each wheel introduces a construction only when it fits naturally.

3.4

भवान् गृहे पुस्तकं पठति।

Bhavān gṛhe pustakaṃ paṭhati. — You read a book at home.

3.5

भवान् अद्य गृहे पुस्तकं पठति।

Bhavān adya gṛhe pustakaṃ paṭhati. — You read a book at home today.

Now rotate bhavān to aham, and paṭhati automatically becomes paṭhāmi.

Rotate gṛhe to vanam with a motion verb, and the correct destination form is generated.

You learn cases, agreement and verb conjugation by seeing their consequences. Only explain the rule when you want to understand why something changed.

## One extra feature I'd keep

Each wheel should offer a tiny “Why did that change?” reveal.

For instance:

Bhavān paṭhati → Ahaṃ paṭhāmi

Why?

Bhavān takes a third-person verb. Aham takes a first-person verb. The change in the ending tells you who's acting.

No compulsory tables, no separate grammar lessons, no endless classification.

My recommendation: four wheels, progressively deeper variants, with grammar learned through transformations. Keep the original 15-family material as reusable patterns inside these four rather than losing it. That makes the system simpler for the learner without sacrificing what it can generate. Yes. This is the right architecture. Four wheels, unlimited packs.

The wheel determines what grammatical skill you're practising. The pack determines what you're talking about. The level determines how sophisticated the Sanskrit becomes.

So rather than building a new wheel for Vedānta, family conversations, classroom Sanskrit, or Trika, you simply load those subjects into the same four engines.

And here's the particularly powerful part: you could master the same grammatical construction across ten completely different subjects, without ever opening a grammar textbook.

## 4. Levels belong to wheels, not packs

This is a crucial distinction.

A learner progresses through Action 3.1 → 3.2 → 3.3 → 3.4, regardless of whether they're studying Family or Trika.

| Level | Grammar introduced     | Example of learning         |
| ----- | ---------------------- | --------------------------- |
| 3.1   | Simple actions         | I study.                    |
| 3.2   | Person changes         | You study. She studies.     |
| 3.3   | Objects and cases      | She studies a book.         |
| 3.4   | Locations and movement | She studies at home.        |
| 3.5   | Time and frequency     | She studies every day.      |
| 3.6   | Wants and purposes     | She wants to study.         |
| 3.7   | Past and future        | She studied yesterday.      |
| 3.8   | Sequence and causation | After studying, she writes. |

A pack may have its own vocabulary difficulty and thematic progression, but it shouldn't create a duplicate grammar syllabus.

For example:

- Action 3.4 + Family Pack: My brother reads at home.
- Action 3.4 + Classroom Pack: The student studies in the classroom.
- Action 3.4 + Trika Pack: I study the Śiva Sūtras in Varanasi.

All three reinforce locations and case endings.

### Mastery and exploration

Progress should be stored on three independent axes:

1. Grammar mastery: Can you use the construction accurately across different packs?
2. Vocabulary familiarity: Which pack words do you recognise?
3. Conversation fluency: Can you respond without revealing the answer?

You never need to complete every pack to level up. If a student struggles with Action 3.4, they can practise locations in the Conversation Wheel, then return without losing their place.

The system should occasionally recommend changing packs to test transfer: mastering paṭhāmi with Sanskrit vocabulary is not quite the same as recognising it in an unfamiliar sentence about family.

## 7. Making packs genuinely modular

A pack shouldn't be a collection of complete sentences. It should contain reusable vocabulary and semantic relationships which the four wheels can incorporate.

For instance, a Family Pack contains:

```

{
  "id": "family",
  "version": "1.0.0",
  "name": "Family",
  "modules": [
    "people",
    "relationships",
    "daily-life",
    "interests"
  ],
  "lexemes": [
    {
      "id": "mother",
      "lemma": "मातृ",
      "iast": "mātṛ",
      "meaning": "mother",
      "kind": "noun",
      "gender": "feminine",
      "declension": "r-stem"
    },
    {
      "id": "read",
      "root": "पठ्",
      "iast": "paṭh",
      "meaning": "read/study",
      "kind": "verb",
      "class": 1,
      "pada": "parasmaipada"
    }
  ],
  "topics": [
    "Who is in your family?",
    "Where does your mother live?",
    "What does your brother study?"
  ]
}

```

The application already knows how to inflect compatible words and assemble them according to wheel grammar. The pack supplies the nouns, verbs, relationships and thematic constraints.

Important: the sample is a content manifest, not a licence to generate arbitrary combinations. The engine must check agreement, selectional compatibility, argument structure and available verified forms. A new pack shouldn't need its own bespoke generator.

# Svatantrya Sanskrit Wheels: Implementation Directive

## Core principle

Build **four universal, five-ring Sanskrit practice wheels**: Conversation, Expression, Action, and Description.

Separate the system into three independent concepts:

- **Wheel:** What linguistic operation the learner practises.
- **Level:** What grammatical complexity is permitted.
- **Pack:** What vocabulary, themes, situations and semantic relationships populate the wheel.

Do not create a separate wheel for every grammatical topic or vocabulary domain.

## Integration

Preserve the existing Sanskrit Wheel interface, progress data, and validated lesson content. Refactor the existing 15-family, 60-level material into four wheel families where appropriate. Preserve old identifiers through a migration map rather than deleting progress.

### Functional requirements

1. Each exercise exposes five meaningful rotating rings.
2. Every ring change regenerates valid Sanskrit, IAST and English.
3. Levels progressively introduce grammar within each wheel.
4. Packs are versioned, independently installable and reusable across all compatible wheels.
5. The same level can be practised using different packs.
6. Switching packs does not reset grammar mastery.
7. Progress tracks grammatical skills, lexical familiarity and conversation performance separately.
8. Learners may explore other wheels and packs without abandoning their daily focus.
9. An optional "Why did that change?" reveal explains the exact grammatical transformation.
10. A pack-creation workflow can propose new packs from conversation topics, but requires human review and linguistic validation before publishing.

### Generation requirements

Use structured grammatical representations, not unrestricted string concatenation. Enforce agreement, declension, conjugation, case assignment, semantic compatibility and sandhi as applicable.

Support both curated forms and verified morphological generation.

Every example must have aligned Sanskrit, IAST and English. Provide relevant provenance for textual vocabulary and never invent quotations.

### Curriculum requirements

Introduce sentence construction through meaningful patterns. Prefer learning person, number, tense, case and agreement by seeing forms transform across the same sentence.

Allow cross-wheel recommendations where an existing construction would help the learner overcome a difficulty.

### Initial deliverable

Produce a complete working prototype of the four wheels with the six starter packs: About Me, Family, Classroom, Travel, Vedānta and Trika.

Implement at least three progressively more capable levels per wheel, five rotating rings, reveal controls, a daily-focus mode, persistent local progress and a pack loading interface.

Validate generated examples, test every selectable combination in the initial curated domain, and document how new packs and levels can be added without changing core UI code.

Do not replace the existing Svatantrya website or deploy unreviewed Sanskrit content automatically.

That's the system I'd commit to: four wheels, expandable levels, swappable packs, one shared grammar engine. It keeps the learner's experience wonderfully simple while leaving almost unlimited room for richer Sanskrit.
